\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Multi-hop QA}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) combines a language model with external
evidence retrieved before answer generation
\cite{lewis2020retrieval,gao2024ragsurvey}. Dense retrievers such as DPR
represent queries and passages in a shared embedding space
\cite{karpukhin2020dense}, while subsequent work improves evidence aggregation,
adaptive retrieval, and hierarchical organization
\cite{izacard2021fid,asai2024selfrag,sarthi2024raptor}. Nevertheless, most RAG
systems still provide evidence primarily as independent textual passages rather
than as an explicit reasoning structure.

Multi-hop benchmarks such as HotpotQA \cite{yang2018hotpotqa} and MuSiQue
\cite{trivedi2022musique} show that retrieving individually relevant passages
is insufficient when an answer depends on several connected reasoning steps.
This limitation is particularly important in legal QA, where a conclusion may
depend on a sequence of statutory conditions, intermediate events, exceptions,
and consequences.

\subsection{Graph-based and Causal RAG}

Graph-based retrieval organizes evidence through relations among entities,
events, or passages. GraphRAG uses graph structure to aggregate information
across related sources \cite{edge2024graphrag}, while Think-on-Graph performs
explicit graph traversal to construct traceable reasoning paths
\cite{sun2024thinkongraph}. CausalRAG incorporates causal relations into
retrieval and reasoning \cite{wang2025causalrag}, and CC-RAG constructs causal
chains for structured multi-hop inference \cite{parekh2025ccrag}.

Our setting differs in that graph edges represent normative
condition--consequence dependencies extracted from statutory text. They are
used to organize legal reasoning and should not be interpreted as statistical
causal effects estimated from observational data. Moreover, graph reachability
alone does not define the semantics of the associated legal rules.

\subsection{Legal QA and Counterfactual Reasoning}

Legal benchmarks and domain-specific models, including LexGLUE, CaseHOLD,
LegalBench, and LEGAL-BERT, demonstrate both the distinct characteristics of
legal language and the difficulty of structured legal reasoning
\cite{chalkidis2022lexglue,zheng2021casehold,guha2023legalbench,
chalkidis2020legalbert}. These studies motivate systems that combine legal
evidence retrieval with explicit and auditable reasoning structures.

Counterfactual multi-hop QA further shows that a model may produce a correct
answer while relying on a disconnected or inappropriate reasoning chain
\cite{guo2023counterfactual}. In structural causal models, however, an
intervention is not equivalent to deleting a node from a graph
\cite{pearl2009causality}. A node-deletion test only examines topological
reachability, whereas $do(X_m=0)$ fixes the mediator variable $X_m$ to false
and replaces its structural mechanism before descendant variables are
recomputed. This distinction is essential when evaluating the role of an
intermediate legal event.

\subsection{Three-Valued and Linear-Algebraic Logic Programming}

Three-valued logic distinguishes true, false, and an undefined or unknown state
\cite{kleene1952metamathematics}. Fitting's Kripke--Kleene semantics provides a
fixed-point interpretation of logic programs under partial information
\cite{fitting1985kripke}. Sato et al. encode two- and three-valued logic-program
semantics in vector spaces \cite{sato2020threevalued}, while Sakama et al. use
matrix and tensor representations for definite, disjunctive, and normal logic
programs \cite{sakama2021tensor}.

These studies motivate our sparse body/head incidence formulation. Conjunction
is represented by multiple literals within one rule, whereas alternative
disjunctive support is represented by independently activated rules with the
same consequent. The formulation provides a linear-algebraic view of the rule
system, but our runtime evaluates the corresponding sparse rules symbolically
to a fixed point rather than performing dense matrix or tensor multiplication.

\subsection{Research Gap}

Existing RAG approaches emphasize semantic retrieval, graph-based approaches
emphasize structural connectivity, and linear-algebraic logic programming
provides formal rule semantics. However, limited work combines these directions
in a legal QA framework that jointly:

\begin{itemize}
    \item retrieves and ranks statutory multi-hop reasoning paths;
    \item represents incomplete legal states using three-valued rule semantics;
    \item handles conjunction within rules and alternative disjunctive
    mechanisms across rules;
    \item distinguishes topological node deletion from structural intervention;
    and
    \item selectively commits verified decisions before answer generation.
\end{itemize}

LegalSCM-RAG addresses this gap by integrating causal-path retrieval,
three-valued fixed-point inference, query-aware structural verification, and an
audited commit gate for Vietnamese multi-hop legal question answering.
