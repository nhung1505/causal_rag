\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Multi-Hop Question Answering}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) was introduced as an architecture that
combines the generative capabilities of language models with an external
knowledge memory \cite{lewis2020rag}. Rather than relying solely on
knowledge encoded in model parameters, RAG retrieves passages relevant to a
query and uses them as context for generation. This mechanism allows the
knowledge source to be updated without retraining the entire model, while
also making it possible to trace answers back to source documents.

Most basic RAG pipelines perform passage-level retrieval and rank candidates
according to the similarity between query and document representations. This
approach assumes that one or more highly ranked passages contain sufficient
information to answer the question. However, this assumption does not always
hold for multi-hop questions. In such cases, the first piece of evidence may
identify an intermediate event, while a second piece of evidence links that
intermediate event to the final conclusion. If each piece of evidence is
ranked independently, the system may retrieve the correct information
fragments but fail to recognize their reasoning order.

HotpotQA is one of the influential benchmarks for multi-hop question
answering, in which a system must not only provide an answer but also
identify the supporting facts used \cite{yang2018hotpotqa}. Research on this
type of task shows that the quality of retrieving individual passages and
the quality of recovering the complete reasoning chain are distinct
problems. This distinction is particularly important in the legal domain,
where the order in which rules are applied may determine the meaning of the
conclusion: an initial condition gives rise to an intermediate legal state,
and that state subsequently triggers another legal effect.

In addition to retrieval structure, the quality of query and document
representations directly affects the ability to identify the correct rule or
event. BGE-M3 supports multiple representation and retrieval mechanisms in a
single multilingual model, including dense retrieval, sparse retrieval, and
multi-vector retrieval \cite{chen2024bgem3}. In this study, BGE-M3 is used to
encode Vietnamese rules and events. Nevertheless, embeddings provide only a
semantic-similarity signal; the multi-hop reasoning structure must still be
recovered through the graph and causal-path expansion.

\subsection{Graph-Based RAG and Path-Based Retrieval}

Graph-based RAG replaces or augments a repository of independent passages
with a graph representation, in which nodes describe entities, events,
concepts, or documents and edges describe relations among the nodes. This
structure enables retrieval based not only on direct similarity to the query
but also on neighboring nodes, paths, and communities in the graph.

GraphRAG organizes extracted knowledge into a graph and uses community-level
summaries to support queries that require broad information synthesis
\cite{edge2024graphrag}. The primary objective of this approach is to
exploit the global structure of the corpus rather than retrieve only the
passages nearest to the query. HippoRAG uses a graph as a form of associative
memory, allowing signals to propagate from concepts activated by the query
to nodes connected over multiple hops \cite{gutierrez2024hipporag}. These
studies show that graphs can improve the ability to connect pieces of
evidence that are not simultaneously highly similar to a question.

Nevertheless, graph connectivity is not automatically equivalent to the
validity of a reasoning chain. A path may exist because two events occur in
the same document, concern the same entity, or are connected by a relation
that is not causally directed. This distinction is even more important in
the legal domain because a system must distinguish among reference
relations, co-occurrence within a statutory provision, and
condition--effect relations.

This study uses a heterogeneous graph in which causal paths are restricted
to \texttt{CAUSES} edges derived from normalized legal rules. Each hop in a
path must be linked to at least one rule and its corresponding statutory
basis. Thus, the principal retrieval unit is not merely a node or passage,
but an ordered chain:

\[
v_0 \xrightarrow{r_1} v_1
    \xrightarrow{r_2} \cdots
    \xrightarrow{r_h} v_h,
\]

where \(v_i\) is an event and \(r_i\) is the legal rule supporting the
transition. This representation does not assert that every edge in the
graph is an empirical causal relation; it encodes only the normative
direction of inference from condition to effect according to the corpus in
use.

\subsection{Structural Causal Models and Counterfactual Reasoning}

Structural Causal Models provide a formal language for representing
variables, structural mechanisms, and the results of interventions
\cite{pearl2009causality}. The key distinction between intervention and
observation is the \(do(\cdot)\) operator. When \(do(M=m)\) is performed, the
value of variable \(M\) is forcibly assigned and the ordinary mechanism that
generates \(M\) is replaced. Descendant variables are then re-inferred under
the remaining structural mechanisms. This interpretation differs from
merely deleting a node from a graph and checking whether a path remains
between two other nodes.

Recent studies have begun to combine causal reasoning with RAG. CausalRAG
uses causal structure to support retrieval and reasoning rather than relying
solely on semantic associations \cite{wang2025causalrag}. Research on
causal-counterfactual RAG further investigates the use of counterfactual
claims or contextual changes to examine answer stability
\cite{khadilkar2025causalcounterfactualrag}. CF-RAG reflects the growing
interest in counterfactual reasoning within RAG systems
\cite{qin2026cfrag}. These works suggest that incorporating causal structure
can help distinguish evidence that is merely semantically correlated from
evidence that plays a role in a reasoning mechanism.

The present study has a different scope from causal discovery or causal
effect estimation. The system neither learns edge directions from
observational data nor estimates an average effect quantity. Instead, the
condition--effect direction is obtained from normalized legal rules.
LegalSCM uses these rules as deterministic mechanisms to validate factual
paths and simulate assigning a mediator the \(\mathrm{FALSE}\) state.
Consequently, the counterfactual result of LegalSCM is meaningful only within
the scope of the normative model and the input corpus.

Another distinction of this study is its query-based claim analysis. The
system does not perform an intervention for every question. For standard
causal questions, LegalSCM primarily checks whether the factual path can be
activated. For claims about a direct edge, the system checks edge structure
rather than inferring that a two-hop path is equivalent to a direct
relation. A hard intervention is interpreted only when the query actually
refers to removing a mediator or assuming its absence.

\subsection{Question Answering and Reasoning in the Legal Domain}

Existing legal benchmarks cover a wide range of problems, from
classification and outcome prediction to authority retrieval and question
answering. CaseHOLD evaluates the ability to select the appropriate holding
in legal cases, thereby emphasizing the need to distinguish among legally
plausible options with similar content \cite{zheng2021casehold}. LegalBench
brings together numerous tasks designed to evaluate different legal
capabilities of language models, including rule identification, rule
application, and scenario-based reasoning \cite{guha2023legalbench}.

LegalBench-RAG, meanwhile, focuses more explicitly on the role of retrieval
in legal applications \cite{alami2024legalbenchrags}. This research
direction aligns with the practical requirement that legal answers be tied
to source documents or statutory provisions. However, retrieving the correct
document does not guarantee that the model has applied the rules in the
correct order. A citation may also identify the correct provision without
directly supporting the generated conclusion.

Compared with these benchmarks, the evaluation set used in this study is
narrower in terms of the legal system covered but focuses specifically on
multi-hop reasoning structures in the Vietnamese Penal Code. Each question
is linked to gold rules, gold events, a gold path, a gold answer, and gold
citations. The question types include forward reasoning, reverse reasoning,
bridge-event identification, chain explanation, direct-edge checking,
branching, and convergence.

However, the current evaluation set is generated from the same source graph
and has not undergone a complete review process by legal experts.
Accordingly, this study does not treat it as a normative legal benchmark or
as representative of every form of reasoning in the Penal Code. It is used
as a controlled testbed for evaluating path recovery, claim verification,
and answer generation from the graph.

\subsection{Positioning of This Study}

This study does not regard any individual component---embedding retrieval,
graph traversal, an SCM, or extractive answer generation---as an entirely
novel contribution. The contribution lies in how these components are
integrated and scoped for legal reasoning:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Rules and events are represented simultaneously in vector memory
    and a heterogeneous legal causal graph.

    \item Retrieval results are organized into ordered causal paths, with
    each hop linked to a rule and statutory basis.

    \item Query analysis determines the claim type and answer format without
    using benchmark labels.

    \item LegalSCM validates factual paths and hard interventions within the
    scope of the normative model, while node-deletion reachability is
    retained as a separate baseline.

    \item The final answer is generated according to the answer intent and
    uses citations only from rules that actually participate in the selected
    reasoning path.

    \item Structural SCM and path ablation are evaluated in a paired setup to
    distinguish their effects on quality from their computational cost and
    interpretability.
\end{enumerate}

The empirical comparison shows that structural SCM and path ablation attain
identical quality metrics on the current benchmark. Rather than supporting a
claim of SCM superiority, this result indicates that the present question
set is not sufficiently sensitive to distinguish the two intervention
semantics. This finding also motivates the construction of samples with
ground truth for the factual world, mediator intervention, and
counterfactual outcome in future work.
